Why test your screen at all?

Most display problems are invisible during normal use. A single dead pixel hides inside photos and videos, backlight bleed only shows up in dark scenes, and uneven brightness is easy to miss until you open a plain document at night. Manufacturers also work to pixel tolerances, which means a small number of faulty pixels may not qualify for a warranty replacement unless you can point to them clearly. A five-minute test with plain full-screen colours gives you that evidence while the screen is still inside its return window. It is also the fastest way to check a used monitor before you pay for it: open this page on the machine, run through the colours, and the panel has nowhere to hide.

The black screen test

A pure black full-screen page is the most revealing test of all. On an OLED screen, true black means the pixels switch off completely, so any glowing patch tells you something is wrong. On an LCD or LED panel, the black test exposes backlight bleed along the edges, clouding in the corners, and the grey haze that shows the backlight is uneven. Dim the room lights, let your eyes adjust for a few seconds, and look slowly across the whole panel. Bright pinpoints that stay in one place are usually stuck or dead pixels showing against the dark background. The black screen test is also handy day to day: it turns a second monitor into a dark backdrop for video calls and helps you clean a screen without mistaking dust for damage.

The white screen test

Where black hides faults, white exposes them. A full-screen white page makes dead pixels appear as tiny dark dots, shows up yellowing or dirty patches on older panels, and reveals uneven brightness between the centre and the edges. It is also the best background for cleaning: smudges, dust and hair stand out clearly on white, so you can wipe the surface properly without pressing too hard. Many people also use the white screen test as a soft light for video calls, as a lightbox for tracing drawings, or to check a phone screen before selling it. If a pixel looks dark on white but glows on black, it is stuck rather than dead, and sometimes gentle use over a few days brings it back.

Colour screens: red, green and blue

Every pixel on your screen is made of red, green and blue sub-pixels, so testing each colour on its own is the most precise way to find faults. A sub-pixel can fail in just one colour, which means the pixel looks fine on white but wrong on a single colour background. Run the red screen test and look for dots that appear dark or discoloured, then do the same with the green screen test and the blue screen test. Green is usually the easiest colour for spotting problems because the human eye is most sensitive to it. Together, the three colour pages complete the picture that black and white start, and the whole set takes only a couple of minutes to run through.

Dead pixels, stuck pixels and hot pixels

These three terms are often mixed up. A dead pixel receives no power and stays black on every background. A stuck pixel is locked on one colour — always red, always green or always blue — and shows up as a bright dot on a black screen. A hot pixel is stuck on white and is easiest to see against dark colours. The full-screen tests on this site let you tell them apart in seconds: note where the dot is, then change the background colour and watch how it behaves. Stuck pixels occasionally recover on their own or after the panel warms up; dead pixels are permanent, which is exactly why it is worth finding them early, while a return or warranty claim is still simple.

A simple five-minute routine

Here is the routine we recommend for any new or second-hand screen. First, clean the display so dust does not confuse the results. Second, open the black page in a dim room and scan slowly for bleed, clouding and bright dots. Third, switch to white and look for dark dots, patches and uneven brightness. Fourth, run red, green and blue, a few seconds each, watching for pixels that change behaviour between colours. Finally, if you found anything, photograph it and check the seller's or manufacturer's pixel policy while the screen is still returnable. Five minutes, six pages, and you know your display properly.
